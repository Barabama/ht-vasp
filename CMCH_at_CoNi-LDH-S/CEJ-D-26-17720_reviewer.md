Abstract:

The synergistic regulation of interface engineering and defect engineering is pivotal for
overcoming the sluggish ion diffusion kinetics in supercapacitors and realizing
high–performance electrode materials. A novel strategy is proposed to construct a
nickel–cobalt layered double hydroxide (NiCo–LDH) with rapid reaction kinetics by
simultaneously introducing F–doping and establishing a p–p heterojunction. The
synthesized electrode material features a hierarchical nanoflower like architecture,
where the interlaced nanosheets facilitate the construction of active interfaces and the
establishment of built–in electric field (IEF). Experimental characterization and DFT
modeling further demonstrate that F–doping modulates the electronic environment to
effectively boost carrier density. Meanwhile, the IEF at the p–p interface, induced by
the work function difference (4.57 eV → 5.23 eV), provides a potent driving force for the
directional migration of holes, thereby optimizing the charge transfer energy barrier.
Benefiting from such synergistic electronic regulation, the prepared electrode material
displays an impressive specific capacitance value of 3442 F g⁻¹ (1 A g⁻¹) and maintains
exceptional rate capability. This work yields a profound understanding of the intrinsic
mechanism of the synergistic interplay between F–doping and heterojunction
interfaces, offering a valuable strategy for developing superior energy storage
materials.

1. Introduction
   Driven by energy crises and environmental concerns, developing efficient energy storage systems
   is crucial for scaling renewable energy [1]. Supercapacitors, distinguished by superior power density,
   rapid charge–discharge rates, superior cyclability, as well as exemplary safety, have emerged as
   favorable candidates in the advanced energy storage landscape [2]. Nevertheless, their widespread

application remains severely constrained by an intrinsic deficiency in energy density [3]. Since active
materials dictate supercapacitor performance, current research prioritizes designing novel electrodes
with high capacity, excellent conductivity, and robust stability. Aligned with this design philosophy,
transition metal compounds endowed with abundant redox–active sites are viewed as ideal building
blocks for next–generation high–performance supercapacitors, owing to their ability to deliver
substantial Faradaic pseudocapacitance [4].
Thanks to their structural advantages, LDHs stand out among transition metal compounds as ideal
candidates for high-performance energy storage. Benefiting from their distinctive 2D layered
structure and large interlayer spacing, these materials provide stable support that facilitates rapid ion
transport and exchange during redox reactions [5]. Within the diverse LDH family, bimetallic NiCo–
LDH distinguishes itself from monometallic counterparts or combinations containing inactive
components (e.g., Zn/Ni–based LDHs) by demonstrating ultrahigh theoretical specific capacity and
rich redox chemistry [6]. This superiority stems primarily from the synergistic interaction between
Ni and Co species, where overlapping potential windows facilitate continuous multistep redox
reactions, thereby effectively multiplying active sites and imparting exceptional pseudocapacitive
properties. Despite these merits, LDHs frequently suffer from restricted electron transport under
high–rate or long–cycling conditions, stemming from intrinsic sluggish charge transfer kinetics that
hampers the directional migration of carriers [7].
Coupling LDHs with transition metal sulfides (TMSs) to construct heterostructures represents a
potent strategy to surmount these electronic transport limitations, leveraging the high conductivity
and complementary electronic structures of TMSs. Among various TMSs, cobalt sulfide (CoS)
garners significant attention in the supercapacitor domain for its metallic–like conductivity, superior
chemical stability, and prolific redox–active sites [8]. Although morphological engineering has
yielded some improvements in its electrochemical behavior [9,10], single–component CoS remains
constrained by limited interfacial charge modulation capabilities and reaction kinetics. Crucially, the
built–in electric field (IEF) established within the interfacial regions of heterojunctions electrodes
serves as a pivotal physical mechanism for governing carrier behavior. By driving interfacial charge
redistribution and accelerating charge transfer, the IEF provides a theoretical cornerstone for high–
performance interface design. Upon contact, differences in Fermi levels or energy band structures
between heterogeneous components spontaneously induce charge redistribution, culminating in a

stable interfacial IEF [2,11]. Consequently, in situ coupling of CoS with Ni Co–LDH to form a
heterojunction system not only enhances the electron transport capacity of LDHs but also
synergistically accelerates reaction kinetics.
Beyond structural interface engineering, heteroatom doping offers a precise avenue for modulating
the electronic structure at the heterointerface. Among potential dopants, the fluorine (F) atom,
characterized by its extreme electronegativity, can significantly alter local potential distributions and
energy level alignments without compromising host lattice stability [12]. This augmented interfacial
driving force facilitates directional charge migration and lowers the energy barrier for charge
transport, thereby intrinsically accelerating reaction kinetics [13]. Ultimately, the synergistic
interplay between F–doping and heterostructure engineering optimizes the interfacial electronic
structure while sustaining stable electrochemical behavior sustaining stable electrochemical
behavior, realizing synchronous enhancements in both energy and power densities.
Guided by these principles, the current work presents an F–doped surface–decorated F–CoS
@NiCoLDH/NF heterojunction electrode fabricated on a nickel foam (NF) scaffold by employing a
sequential electrodeposition route alongside chemical immersion strategy. By synergistically
introducing band matching effects and IEF driving forces at the heterointerface, the resulting
architecture establishes continuous, high–efficiency charge transport channels within the electrode,
significantly optimizing reaction kinetics and ion diffusion. Moreover, density functional theory
(DFT) simulations clarify that F–doping effectively tunes and intensifies the IEF effect at the
heterointerface by regulating the energy level alignment and Fermi level positions on both sides,
which synergistically lowers the interfacial charge transfer barrier. Driven by the interfacial
electronic synergy, the F–CS@NCL/NF//AC HSC delivers 114 W h kg-1 at 750 W kg-1,
demonstrating its huge potential for advanced energy storage.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials
Analytical-grade Co (NO3)2·6H2O, Ni (NO3)2·6H2O, thiourea and NH4F were procured from
Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd. and used as received.
2.2. Materials Synthesis
2.2.1. Preparation of NiCoLDH/NF (NCL/NF)

4 mmol of Co (NO3)2·6H2O and Ni (NO3)2·6H2O were dissolved in 100 mL DIW and stirred to
dissolve. NF acted as the working electrode, coupled with a Pt wire counter electrode and an
Hg/HgO reference electrode. A potentiostatic method was executed applying a fixed potential of –2
V lasting 20 min. The resulting samples were washed thoroughly before being dried under vacuum
for 12 h.
2.2.2. Synthesis of CoS@NiCoLDH/NF (CS@NCL/NF)
2 mmol of Co (NO3)2·6H2O and 2 mmol of thiourea were dissolved in 100 mL DIW and stirred to
dissolve. CV electrodeposition was then performed on the NCL/NF electrode (–1.2 to –0.2 V). After
washing thrice with DIW, the sample was dried under vacuum at 60℃ for 12 h to yield
CS@NCL/NF. For the synthesis of the pure CoS sample, bare NF was used as the working electrode
while maintaining other parameters constant.
2.2.3. Synthesis of F–CoS@NiCoLDH/NF (F–CS@NCL/NF)
The as–prepared CS@NCL/NF was immersed into purified water containing 2 mmol of NH4F.
The F–CS@NCL/NF product was yielded after 3 hours of immersion.The F–CoS samples were
prepared by substituting the precursor with CoS and repeating the aforementioned immersion
procedure.
2.2.4. Synthesis of the F–CoS@NiCoLDH/NF Hybrid Supercapacitor (F–CS@NCL//AC HSC)
A mixture of AC, acetylene black, and PTFE (8:1:1 weight ratio) was thoroughly ground and
coated onto NF. Vacuum dry at 60 °C for 6 h. By sandwiching the F–CS@NCL/NF cathode along
with the activated carbon anode on opposite sides of a cellulose separator followed by compression
encapsulation.

2.5. DFT Calculation
First–principles investigations relying on DFT were executed using the Materials Studio package
software suite which was primarily employed for the construction of molecular models and the
precise optimization of crystalline structures. Throughout the geometric optimization, the
convergence criteria were strictly defined by interatomic force fluctuations below 0.03 eV/Å
alongside an energy variation threshold lower than 10–5 eV. The optimization process was only
considered converged when both requirements were simultaneously satisfied by the calculation
results. For the crystalline structures of NCL and CoS, the corresponding supercell models were
constructed via slicing treatments, and accurate lattice constants were subsequently obtained upon
the completion of the optimization. First–principles simulations were conducted using the VASP
code, adopting the projector augmented wave (PAW) method and a plane–wave basis with a 450 eV
energy cutoff. Based on this theoretical framework, we systematically investigated the differential
charge density, density of states (DOS), and band structures of the materials. These investigations
aimed to provide a fundamental and in–depth explanation regarding the electronic properties as well
as the internal charge distribution patterns of the materials.
3 Results and Discussion
3.1. Morphology and structure characterizations
The formation pathway for the F–CS@NCL/NF heterojunction as followed Fig. 1a. Initially, the
NCL/NF nanostructure was grown on a NF substrate through potentiostatic deposition utilizing Co
(NO3)2·6H2O and Ni (NO3)2·6H2O. This structure served as a scaffold for the deposition of CoS via
CV to establish the CS@NCL/NF heterointerface. Ultimately, heteroatom doping was accomplished
through the introduction of ammonium fluoride to yield the targeted F–CS@NCL/NF product. In a
comparative experiment, pure CoS/NF was synthesized by substituting the working electrode with
bare NF. Notably, the morphology remains largely unaltered after F–doping, signifying that the
doping strategy optimizes the electronic configuration without compromising the inherent crystal
growth mechanism.
SEM images reveal that NCL/NF features ultra–thin cluster microspheres assembled from
nanosheets (Fig. 1b and Fig. 1c), providing abundant anchors for active species. Upon CoS

introduction, the morphology evolves into interlaced vertical nanosheet arrays (Fig. 1d and Fig. 1e).
Compared with pure CoS/NF (Fig. S1) also presents similar sheet–like characteristics, confirming
that the morphology is dominated by the growth habit of CoS/NF [14]. Specifically, the NCL/NF
clusters serve as a skeleton to induce the uniform nucleation of CoS/NF at the initial stage of
deposition. subsequently, CoS/NF extends outward to form well–defined and extended sheet–like
structures covering the NCL/NF surface. This hierarchical architecture provides open ion transport
channels and ensures intimate interfacial contact, facilitating efficient charge transfer [4]. The well–
preserved morphology after F–incorporation (Fig. 1d, Fig. 1e, Fig. 1f and Fig. 1g) implies that the
doping process exerts a negligible impact on the intrinsic crystallization pathways, ensuring
consistent nucleation and growth kinetics [2]. The microscopic architecture of the F–CS@NCL/NF
composite was elucidated utilizing TEM analysis. In Fig. 1h, NCL is visualized alongside CoS, well–
integrated heterostructure. Lattice fringes with d–spacings of 0.294 nm and 0.386 nm are observed,
corresponding to the (100) and (006) planes of CoS and NCL, respectively (Fig. 1i). The results
corroborate the XRD findings and support the existence of a CoS/NCL heterointerface. Furthermore,
EDS mapping highlights the even dispersion of Co, Ni, F, S, C, and O elements across the sample,
confirming the successful preparation of F–CS@NCL/NF.

valence bands toward the Fermi level while substantially enhancing the DOS. Such electronic
structure tuning effectively facilitates the dynamic attachment and detachment of ions from the
electrolyte onto the electrode interface.
We characterized the electrical properties through Mott–Schottky (M–S) measurements, aiming to
demonstrate the existence of the p–p type heterostructure. Figs. S2d and Fig. S2e reveals that the M–
S plots of both NCL/NF and CoS/NF exhibit distinct negative slopes that align with the typical
behavior of p–type semiconductors, thereby confirming the establishment of a p–p heterojunction
[16]. Furthermore, as indicated in Figs S2f-S2h, F–CoS/NF, CS@NCL/NF, and F–CS@NCL/NF
also display negative slopes, adhering to the fundamental physics of semiconductor contacts. M–S
plots (Fig. 2i) confirm F–CS@NCL/NF exhibits the lowest slope absolute value, indicating
maximum carrier density. The resulting transition effectively mitigates the space charge transport
resistance and significantly enhances the hole migration rate, consequently improving the
electrochemical charge storage properties of the material. Verification of the computational outcomes
was performed via CV in conjunction with constant current charge/discharge (GCD) analyses.
Meanwhile, nearly the flat–band potential difference (∣ΔEfb∣) of all samples was observed within the
0.2–0.4 V range (Figs S2d-S2h) indicates the formation of an optimal interfacial potential gradient.

The established potential gradient not only provides sufficient driving force for the directional
transport of holes but also effectively suppresses carrier recombination [17].

We investigated the charge transfer mechanism at the hetero–interface by evaluating the work
functions (Φ) of NCL, CoS, and F–CoS (Figs. S2f, Fig. S2g and Fig S2h). Representing the energy

difference between the Fermi level and the vacuum level, the work functions for the three materials
were determined to be 5.23, 4.57, and 4.78 eV, respectively. Due to the distinct work function
differences between phases, the spontaneous alignment of Fermi levels triggers band bending at the
interface, resulting in the generation of a space charge region and an interfacial potential barrier,
which lays the thermodynamic foundation for the effective establishment of the p–p heterojunction.
As evidenced by the charge distribution model presented in Fig. 2k, NCL serves as an electron
acceptor because of its higher work function, a characteristic that aligns with the classic high–work–
function core and low–work–function shell configuration for p–p heterojunctions [18]. Furthermore,
the work function gradient between F–CoS and CoS further drives electron transfer toward NCL.
This potential difference acts as the core factor that induces interfacial charge redistribution. The
work function gradient (NCL > F–CoS > CoS) induces interfacial charge redistribution, consistent
with the M–S flat–band potential trends.

Raman spectra (Fig. 3c) display characteristic Co–O (556 cm⁻¹) and Ni–O (512 cm⁻¹) modes in

which is consistent with the lattice expansion and enhanced conductivity revealed by XRD and M–S
measurements. Ultimately, the synergistic optimization of vibrational modes and electronic
configurations facilitates accelerated charge transfer kinetics during electrochemical reactions [14].

electronegativity (3.98), effectively elevate the oxidation states of the metal elements by inducing
electron transfer [27]. Concurrently, the Ni 2p spectrum manifests a synchronized modulation trend.
the characteristic peaks of Ni²⁺ and Ni³⁺ exhibit a negative shift of 0.4 eV (Fig. 3g). The observed
spectral shift is ascribed to the accumulation of electrons on the Ni centers. Based on the work
function analysis (Fig. 3k), the built–in electric field drive electrons from the CoS/NF domain to the
NCL/NF domain. Consequently, Ni acts as an electron acceptor, leading to an increased electron
cloud density and a reduced binding energy. The S 2p spectrum (Fig. 3i) further corroborates the
sophisticated regulation of the electronic structure. Additionally, the peak at 168.8 eV is ascribed to
Co–S–O species, stemming from the inevitable surface chemical oxidation of the starting materials
during synthesis or storage processes [20]. Relative to CS@NCL/NF, the S²⁻ signals of F–
CS@NCL/NF exhibit positive shifts of 0.2 eV (2p₁/₂) and 0.4 eV (2p₃/₂), which reflects a reduction in
the electron cloud density at the S sites. This charge redistribution process is driven by the
electronegative induction of F atoms withdraw electrons from Co, subsequently driving electron
migration from S to Co to compensate for the deficiency [27]. Such electron flow, stimulated by the
coupling between F–doping and the heterointerface, not only diminishes the electron density of S but
also accelerating the interfacial reaction kinetics by optimizing the Co coordination environment
[14].

3.4. Density functional theory calculation
DFT calculations were employed to uncover the influence of F-doping and the heterointerface on
electronic structures and kinetics. Fig. 6a compares the total DOS for NCL, CoS, CS@NCL, and F–
CS@NCL, where the results indicate that the electronic synergy between NCL and CoS significantly
enhances the energy level distribution of CS@NCL near the Fermi level, strongly substantiating that
the interfacial effect of the p–p heterojunction can further improve the electronic conductivity of the
material. Crucially, F–CS@NCL displays a higher energy level density near the Fermi level than
CS@NCL, signifying that the interfacial coupling effect of F–doping has effectively accelerated the
charge transfer process. Fig. 6c presents the partial density of states (PDOS) of the electrode
materials. Based on the d band center (εd) theory, the position of the εd for transition metal elements
is intrinsically linked to their adsorption capacity for the medium. Within the established theoretical
framework, a closer εd to the Fermi level generally leads to a more prominent electron–electrode
interaction [35]. Compared with the values of CS@NCL (–0.846 eV), CoS (–1.072 eV), and NCL (–
1.311 eV), the εd of F–CS@NCL (–0.594 eV) is located nearest to the Fermi level. This proximity
demonstrates that the synergy between surface F–doping and the p–p heterojunction enhances the
adsorption capacity of the electrode material for OH⁻ during the electrochemical reaction.
This work performed a detailed calculation of the differential charge density (Fig. 6b) with the
objective of profoundly clarifying the charge transfer magnitude and spatial distribution of F–
CS@NCL. Within this visualization, the blue and yellow regions represent the electron dissipation
zones and accumulation respectively, whereby a clear aggregation of electrons is observed at the
NCL/CoS hetero–interface. This is attributed to the interfacial interaction generated by the
combination of the two components, which can effectively drive the redistribution of interfacial
charges and thus significantly increase the interfacial charge density. Furthermore, the presence of
distinct charge accumulation and dissipation surrounding the F–doping sites signifies the dual
functionality of F–doping in promoting charge carrier transport and improving conductivity while
simultaneously strengthening the coupling between the interfaces. To evaluate the thermodynamic
feasibility F–doping and identify the optimal doping site, this work systematically calculated the

formation energies of fluorine atoms at the Ni and Co sites of NCL as well as the Co sites within the
CoS shell. The formation energy of F–CoS–Co reaches a minimum value of –2.37 eV (Fig. 6d)
which implies that fluorine atoms preferentially occupy the exposed cobalt sites in the CoS shell.
This thermodynamically favorable site exhibits high consistency with the experimental synthesis
route to ensure a precise match between the theoretical model and the structural characteristics of the
experimental samples. Additionally, the CS@NCL heterojunction demonstrates an exceptionally low
formation energy of –5285.03 eV as detailed in Fig. S5. This result signifies that the surface–
decorated architecture possesses superior stability and can form spontaneously to provide a reliable
structural foundation for the subsequent F–doping process.
Dictated by the Sabatier principle, $H_2O$ adsorption energy crucially regulates the overall
electrochemical kinetics. Specifically, within alkaline systems, water molecules must undergo
dissociation to generate H* active intermediates whereby the adsorption strength of this process not
only directly determines the adsorption and desorption kinetics of reaction intermediates but also
profoundly influences the electrochemical reaction energy barriers and the overall kinetic efficiency
[36]. The specific values of water adsorption energy (ΔEH₂O) were calculated in accordance with
Equation 14 [37].
ΔEH₂O = E slab +H₂O – E slab − EH₂O

The DFT calculation results presented in Fig. 6e reveal that the ΔEH₂O values for NCL, CoS,
CS@NCL, and F–CS@NCL are –0.48, –0.59, –0.78 and –1.02 eV respectively. Compared with the
other control samples, F–CS@NCL exhibits more superior thermodynamic adsorption behavior
toward water molecules. This optimized adsorption strength assists in the initial adsorption and
activation of reactant molecules on the electrode surface while simultaneously promoting the
efficient desorption of reaction intermediates to provide critical thermodynamic support for the
efficient and continuous progression of the electrochemical reaction. It is noteworthy that the
absolute value of ΔEH₂O for F–CS@NCL is significantly higher than those of the other samples. This
phenomenon indirectly indicates that its surface may possess more abundant active sites which exerts
a prominent positive effect on accelerating the electrochemical reaction rate and strengthening the
charge transfer kinetics at the electrode–electrolyte interface.

4. Conclusion
In summary, through a dual–step electrodeposition and subsequent soaking–doping strategy, we
successfully construct F–CS@NCL/NF composite electrodes, benefiting from the synergistic
regulation of p–p heterojunctions and F–doping. Multiple characterization results combined with
DFT calculations confirm that the p–p heterojunction formed between CoS/NF and NCL/NF can
achieve directional regulation of charges by virtue of the difference in work function, thus effectively
driving the rearrangement of interfacial charges and improving the charge transfer rate. Concurrently,
F–doping atoms preferentially occupy the cobalt sites of the CoS/NF shell to significantly strengthen
the adsorption capacity of the material for OH– by further optimizing the electronic structure and
narrowing the bandgap. This synergistic modification effect successfully overcomes the performance
bottlenecks of poor electrical conductivity and slow ion diffusion inherent in single components. In
practical application demonstrations, the series–connected device assembled from this material can
drive an LED to emit light continuously for 15 mins, which fully highlights its exceptional energy

storage potential. This synergistic strategy not only offers a fresh perspective for designing high–
performance supercapacitors but also provides valuable insights for interface engineering in broader
electrochemical fields.
